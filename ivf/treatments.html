Diagnosis before menu

We do not sell treatments. We solve problems.

Fertility medicine attracts add-ons. Some are useful for specific patients; many are sold to everyone because they are profitable, not because the evidence supports them. We work the other way round: establish the diagnosis, identify the single biggest obstacle, and treat that first.

That means one change at a time, so we can tell what worked. It means explaining when a cheaper or simpler option is reasonable. And it means saying “we do not know” when the evidence is genuinely uncertain, rather than dressing up a guess as certainty.

The result is a plan you can defend to your family, your faith and your own conscience — not just a invoice with a protocol attached.

What we assess before recommending anything

  • Your age, ovarian reserve and hormone profile.
  • Sperm count, motility, morphology and DNA fragmentation where relevant.
  • Fallopian tube patency and uterine cavity assessment.
  • Previous cycles — what was done, what failed, and why.
  • Recurrent loss, genetic history and family history.
  • Your medical safety, including thyroid, clotting and metabolic health.

No protocol is written before this picture is complete.

The core treatments

Five techniques. One honest explanation each.

Read the limits as carefully as the benefits. A clinic that only tells you the upside is not giving you informed consent.

01

In-vitro fertilisation (IVF)

Foundation

What it is

Your ovaries are stimulated to mature several eggs. The eggs are retrieved, fertilised with sperm in the laboratory, and cultured for five to six days until they reach the blastocyst stage. One embryo is transferred; suitable remaining embryos can be frozen.

Who it is usually for

  • Blocked or removed fallopian tubes.
  • Endometriosis or ovulation disorders.
  • Unexplained infertility after a full work-up.
  • Age-related decline in egg numbers or quality.
  • Couples who need genetic testing of embryos.

What the cycle involves

  • Baseline scan and bloods on day two or three.
  • Eight to twelve days of stimulation injections.
  • Monitoring scans every two to three days.
  • A timed trigger injection, then retrieval under sedation.
  • Fertilisation check the next day; updates on day three and day five.
  • Transfer, luteal support, and a blood test ten to fourteen days later.

The honest limits

IVF does not guarantee a pregnancy. Implantation — whether a good embryo attaches and grows — remains the part we understand least. Not every egg fertilises, not every embryo reaches blastocyst, and not every transfer implants. We plan for one healthy baby at a time and avoid double transfers unless there is a clear clinical reason.

Your nurse’s role: she teaches the injections, checks in after the first few, and is the person you message when the bloating or the waiting becomes frightening.

02

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)

Male factor

What it is

Instead of mixing sperm and eggs and letting fertilisation happen, the embryologist selects a single sperm and injects it directly into a mature egg. Everything else about the cycle is the same as IVF.

Who it is usually for

  • Low sperm count, poor motility or abnormal morphology.
  • Previous cycle with failed or low fertilisation.
  • Sperm retrieved surgically, or frozen before treatment.
  • Cycles where embryos will be genetically tested.

What it changes

ICSI bypasses the steps where sperm would normally bind and penetrate the egg. It can turn a cycle with very poor fertilisation into a viable one. It does not improve egg quality, embryo quality or implantation, and it is not automatically better for mild male factor.

The honest limits

ICSI is more invasive in the laboratory and slightly more expensive. It bypasses some of nature’s selection, so we use it when there is a clear indication rather than as a default upgrade. Long-term outcome data is reassuring, but no clinic can promise that a single injected sperm will produce a healthy embryo.

Your nurse’s role: she explains why ICSI was or was not recommended, in language that does not require a biology degree — and she will revisit the decision with you before the lab starts.

03

PGT-A and PGT-M genetic screening

Genetic

What it is

PGT-A screens an embryo for the wrong number of chromosomes — a common reason for miscarriage and failed implantation. PGT-M looks for a specific inherited condition, such as thalassaemia, sickle cell disease or cystic fibrosis, when both parents are known carriers.

Who it is usually for

  • Recurrent miscarriage or repeated implantation failure.
  • Advanced maternal age or a history of chromosomal loss.
  • A known single-gene condition in the family (PGT-M).
  • Couples who want to reduce the chance of transferring an affected embryo.

What the cycle involves

  • A few cells are biopsied from each blastocyst on day five or six.
  • The embryos are frozen while the genetics laboratory analyses the samples.
  • Results usually return in one to two weeks.
  • A chromosomally suitable embryo is transferred in a later cycle.
  • PGT-M requires genetic counselling and laboratory probe design before stimulation.

The honest limits

PGT-A is a screening test, not a guarantee. Some embryos are “mosaic” — a mix of normal and abnormal cells — and the interpretation is nuanced. False positives and false negatives exist. PGT does not replace prenatal testing, and it does not remove the need for a healthy uterus and a good transfer plan. We discuss each result with you before any decision is made.

Your nurse’s role: results week is its own kind of two-week wait. She schedules the call, makes sure you have time to ask questions, and does not rush you toward a decision on mosaic embryos.

04

Embryo and egg freezing (vitrification)

Preservation

What it is

Vitrification is an ultra-rapid freezing technique that prevents the formation of damaging ice crystals. Embryos or eggs are stored in liquid nitrogen and can be warmed years later for transfer or another cycle.

Who it is usually for

  • Surplus embryos after a fresh cycle.
  • Freeze-all cycles for genetic testing or to reduce ovarian hyperstimulation risk.
  • Fertility preservation before chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery.
  • Egg freezing for people not ready to conceive.

What to expect

Survival after thaw is good but not universal — most embryos survive, some do not, and a small number may not continue to develop. Success depends heavily on the age at which the egg or embryo was frozen and on its quality at freezing.

The honest limits

Storage is a long-term commitment. You will sign a clear consent covering duration, annual fees, and what happens if contact is lost or circumstances change. Religious and legal questions about the status of stored embryos should be discussed openly, and we will involve your faith or ethics framework rather than assume.

Your nurse’s role: she walks you through the storage agreement line by line, diarises the renewal date, and is the person who calls before a frozen transfer to confirm the plan.

05

Time-lapse incubation

Laboratory

What it is

A time-lapse incubator keeps embryos in a stable, undisturbed environment while a camera captures images every few minutes. The embryologist can review the whole sequence of development without opening the incubator and changing its conditions.

Why it can help

  • Fewer disturbances to temperature, pH and humidity.
  • Continuous information about how each embryo divides.
  • Better discussion with you about why one embryo was chosen over another.
  • Useful in complex cases where embryo selection is difficult.

What it does not do

Time-lapse is a monitoring and selection aid. It does not make an embryo healthy, and the evidence that it improves live birth rates across all patients is not definitive. We do not sell it as a guarantee, and we do not use it to replace the judgement of an experienced embryologist.

How we use it

It is included in the VIP Signature programme and available in Serenity when the laboratory team recommends it. If we think it will not change your outcome, we will tell you — and we will not charge you for reassurance disguised as science.

Your nurse’s role: she translates the embryology updates into plain language, so “day five, 4BB” means something you can understand rather than a code that raises your heart rate.

Matching the treatment to the problem

What we would consider, and why.

Blocked or removed tubes IVF Fertilisation happens in the laboratory, bypassing the tubes.

Our ethical line

What we will not do, even if you ask.

Patients often arrive asking for a specific add-on they read about online. Sometimes it is appropriate. Often it is not. Saying no is part of good medicine, and it is one of the reasons our patients trust the recommendations we do make.

We will explain the evidence, the cost and the alternative. If you still want a treatment we believe is not indicated, we will tell you why we are declining — and, where appropriate, help you find a second opinion.

We do not offer

  • Add-ons without reasonable evidence, sold as “just in case”.
  • Guaranteed success or “money-back baby” promises.
  • Routine double-embryo transfer to inflate the pregnancy rate.
  • Reflex ICSI for mild male factor with no clear indication.
  • Unproven immune therapies, intralipids or routine endometrial scratch.
  • Treatment without a diagnosis, a written plan and informed consent.
