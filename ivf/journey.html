Before you commit

A journey designed for people who cannot afford to guess.

Most international fertility journeys are organised around the clinic’s schedule, not yours. You arrive without a plan, spend the first days repeating your history, and discover the real timeline only once you are already there.

We do the opposite. Diagnosis, protocol and pricing are completed before you travel. Your Cairo dates are planned backwards from your cycle, your flight and your family obligations. The only thing left to do when you land is begin.

One coordinator owns the logistics. One consultant owns the medicine. One nurse owns how you feel about all of it. None of them hand you to a stranger halfway through.

  • A written timeline with your expected dates before booking.
  • A single WhatsApp thread with your coordinator and nurse.
  • No payment before your medical plan and quote are approved.
  • A remote follow-up plan that starts before you leave Cairo.

The five phases at a glance

  • 01 · Pre-arrival Remote diagnostics, protocol design, written quote and travel planning — usually two to four weeks before you fly.
  • 02 · VIP arrival Airport transfer, accommodation, and a first clinic visit that confirms the plan rather than starting from scratch.
  • 03 · Stimulation Daily injections with nurse coaching, plus monitoring scans every few days with your consultant.
  • 04 · Retrieval & transfer Theatre day, laboratory updates, and a calm transfer with a written plan for the days after.
  • 05 · Return & follow-up Travel home, structured two-week-wait support, results by call, and a debrief wherever you are.

Typical total: 14–18 days in Cairo, depending on protocol and response.

Phase by phase

What actually happens, in order.

No phase is improvised. Each one has a purpose, a duration, and a named person accountable for it.

01

Phase one · 2–4 weeks before travel

Pre-arrival: diagnosis before departure.

What happens

A fertility consultant reviews your history in a private video consultation. You share whatever investigations you already have — hormone results, scans, semen analysis, previous cycle notes. If something essential is missing, we tell you exactly what to arrange locally, with a written request you can hand to your own doctor.

Only when the picture is complete do we design a protocol, confirm the medication plan and issue a fixed, itemised quote. Your coordinator then builds the travel timeline: which day you fly, which day stimulation starts, and the earliest safe date for your return.

What we need from you

  • Copies of any previous fertility investigations and cycle summaries.
  • Your passport nationality, so we can advise on visa timing.
  • An honest conversation about budget, work commitments and childcare.
  • Any medical conditions, medication or allergies — including the ones you think are irrelevant.
Your nurse, at this phase She hosts a 45-minute emotional intake before any medication arrives: your history, your triggers, your support network, and what you want us to say — and not say — if results are difficult.
02

Phase two · Days 1–3 in Cairo

VIP arrival: landing into a plan, not a queue.

What happens

Your coordinator confirms your arrival time in advance and arranges your airport transfer. Accommodation is booked close to the clinic, with a private entrance and a direct contact who answers in your language. You are not left to negotiate a taxi queue after a long flight.

Your first clinic visit is a confirmation appointment, not a discovery appointment. We repeat the baseline scan and bloods that matter, verify the protocol against today’s results, and adjust only if your body requires it.

What you will have in hand

  • A printed schedule with every appointment, scan and call.
  • The direct numbers for your consultant’s office, coordinator and nurse.
  • A medication delivery plan, including cold-chain handling.
  • A written explanation of what would make us pause or change the cycle.
Your nurse, at this phase She meets you in person before your first injection — not after it. If you have a needle phobia, this is where the written plan is rehearsed, not where it is first mentioned.
03

Phase three · Days 3–12 in Cairo

Stimulation and monitoring: the part that feels endless.

What happens

You inject daily, usually in the evening, for eight to twelve days. Monitoring scans every two or three days track follicle growth and blood hormone levels. Your consultant adjusts the dose based on your response — not on a standard template.

Most days are short appointments and long afternoons. We plan them early so your day is not consumed by waiting. If your response is slower or faster than expected, the timeline moves with it, and your coordinator rebooks travel and accommodation before it becomes a crisis.

What we watch for

  • Ovarian hyperstimulation risk, with a clear plan to reduce it if signs appear.
  • Poor or uneven response, and whether continuing is clinically worthwhile.
  • Medication side effects, including mood changes and sleep disruption.
  • The moment the trigger injection must be timed to the hour.
Your nurse, at this phase She checks in after your first three injections, coaches you by video if you are injecting alone, and answers the 11 p.m. message about a bruise, a headache or a number on a scan report.
04

Phase four · Days 12–19 in Cairo

Retrieval and transfer: the two days that matter most.

Retrieval day

The trigger injection is timed precisely, then retrieval happens under sedation in theatre. You will know the anaesthetist’s name and the expected duration beforehand. Most patients are home within a few hours, with written recovery instructions and a direct number for the evening.

The embryologist calls the next morning with fertilisation results. On day three and day five you receive structured updates — how many embryos are developing, their grade, and what that means for transfer and freezing.

Transfer day

Transfer is short and usually painless, but it is not a small moment emotionally. The appointment is unhurried. You see the embryo image if you want to. You leave with a written plan covering medication, activity, symptoms and exactly who to call.

If a fresh transfer is not advisable — because of hyperstimulation risk, progesterone timing or genetic testing — we tell you before retrieval day, not after. Freezing and a later frozen transfer is a plan, not a failure.

Your nurse, at this phase She calls the evening before retrieval, checks on you the morning after, and sits with you — physically or by phone — when the embryology update is not what you hoped for.
05

Phase five · From day 19, wherever you are

Safe return and remote follow-up.

Going home

Before you fly, you receive a travel fitness check, a medication plan that is legal to carry into your country, and a letter for airport security if you are carrying injections. Your coordinator confirms your flight and your first follow-up call.

The two-week wait is structured rather than endured: an agreed check-in day, guidance on what symptoms do and do not mean, and a results-day plan fixed in advance — including who calls, at what time, and who is with you.

After the result

  • A results call with time built in for questions, not a portal notification.
  • A written summary and scan plan for your local doctor, if you prefer local monitoring.
  • A debrief with your consultant after a negative result — with no pressure to book again.
  • A clear pathway back, with a revised plan rather than a repeat of the same cycle.
Your nurse, at this phase She remains your named contact after discharge. The relationship does not end at the airport, and it does not end with a negative test either.

Honest contingency planning

What happens when the plan has to change.

A journey is only trustworthy if it explains its detours. These are the four most common ones.

01

Your response is slower than expected

What we do

We adjust the dose, extend stimulation by a few days, and rebook your accommodation and return flight before the change becomes an emergency. If continuing is unlikely to be worthwhile, we say so and discuss cancelling the cycle rather than spending more money on a poor response.

02

Hyperstimulation risk rises

What we do

We reduce the trigger risk, freeze all suitable embryos, and plan a frozen transfer in a later cycle. This is a clinical decision made for your safety — and it is explained before it happens, not after.

03

No embryo is suitable for transfer

What we do

You are told by a person, in a scheduled call, with time to ask why. Your consultant reviews the cycle with you and explains what — if anything — we would do differently. There is no invoice for a next round waiting in the same conversation.

04

Flights, work or family intervene

What we do

Your coordinator rebuilds the schedule around the constraint. Some parts of monitoring can be done locally under our consultant’s guidance. What we will not do is pretend a delay is harmless — we will tell you what it means for your cycle.

Practical preparation

What to arrange before you fly.

Small practical decisions made in advance remove a surprising amount of anxiety later.

You do not need to organise your own medical records, chase a clinic for a summary, or guess how much medication to pack. Your coordinator will send a checklist and confirm each item with you before departure.

Your pre-travel checklist

  • Passport valid for at least six months, and visa confirmed if required.
  • Travel insurance that covers medical treatment abroad, in writing.
  • Copies of previous results, prescriptions and cycle summaries.
  • Enough annual leave for the full range, not just the optimistic estimate.
  • A named person at home who knows the dates and can help if plans shift.
  • Payment method confirmed, with your bank warned about international charges.

Your coordinator reviews this list with you line by line in the week before travel.

Journey questions

What patients ask before they book a flight.

Can I start treatment before I arrive in Cairo?

Often, yes. Some patients begin stimulation at home under our consultant’s written protocol and local monitoring, then fly to Cairo for retrieval and transfer. That can shorten your stay to eight to ten days. It depends on your protocol, your local medical support and your comfort with injecting away from us — all of which we discuss before you commit.

Do I need to bring someone with me?

Not necessarily, but most patients are glad of a companion for retrieval day and the days after transfer. If you are travelling alone, tell your nurse. We build a slightly tighter check-in schedule and make sure you are not alone for the parts that feel hardest.

What if my cycle is cancelled partway through?

Cancellation is uncommon but real, usually because of poor response or hyperstimulation risk. Your written agreement sets out exactly what is refundable, what has already been spent on medication and laboratory work, and what can be carried forward. We explain this before you pay, not at the moment it happens.

Can my local doctor handle monitoring at home?

Usually, yes. We send a written monitoring request with the exact scan days and blood tests required, and your consultant reviews the results remotely. This is common for patients who live in the Gulf, Europe or North Africa and want to minimise time away from work.

How much time off work should I plan for?

Plan for the full range rather than the best case: roughly three weeks away, plus a lighter week after transfer. If your protocol allows remote monitoring, that can fall to ten to twelve days in Cairo. Your coordinator will give you a written date range you can take to your employer.
